How to Create an FAQ Page That Actually Reduces Support Tickets

Almost every website has an FAQ page. Far fewer have one that stops the same questions from landing in the support inbox. If yours reads like a list of guesses, it's probably not doing much.

This guide shows you how to create an FAQ page from the questions people really ask. You'll learn how to write answers they can act on, where the page fits in your help center, and how to keep it accurate after launch.

What is an FAQ page? An FAQ page is a single page of short answers to the questions customers ask most often. Its job is to answer those questions faster than contacting support would, and to point readers to fuller help articles when a short answer isn't enough.

Why most FAQ pages don't deflect tickets

Self-service is popular, but it often fails. In a Gartner survey of customers, covered by CRM industry press, only 14% of service issues were fully resolved in self-service. Even for issues customers called "very simple," the figure was just 36%. Yet 73% of customers used self-service at some point in their journey.

The reasons matter more than the numbers. The most common cause of self-service failure, in 43% of cases, was that customers couldn't find content relevant to their issue. And 45% of customers who started in self-service said the company didn't understand what they were trying to do.

That's the FAQ page problem in a nutshell, and government content designers have been blunt about it. The GOV.WALES writing guidance says it avoids FAQs because what users need "is often hidden within an FAQ page." It lists the usual faults:

  • They duplicate content that already exists elsewhere on the site.
  • People search for something specific, not for "FAQs," so they never find the page.
  • Question-shaped headings make it hard to put the most important words first.
  • Answers run longer than they need to and are hard to scan.

None of this means FAQ pages are a bad idea. The Nielsen Norman Group argues that FAQs still deliver real value when the questions reflect what customers actually ask, in their own words. The lesson is that an FAQ page only works when it's built from evidence and kept tidy.

FAQ pages after Google retired FAQ rich results

Many teams once wrote FAQ pages partly to win extra space in search results. That reason is gone. In August 2023, Google limited FAQ rich results to well-known, authoritative government and health websites. According to Google Search Central's changelog, the feature stopped appearing in Google Search altogether from May 7, 2026.

So write for readers, not for rich results. You don't need to rush to remove existing FAQ markup, either: Google has said structured data it doesn't use causes no problems for Search. Your FAQ page can still rank in ordinary results when it clearly answers what people search for.

The six steps below show you how.

Step 1: Collect the questions customers really ask

Don't start with a blank page and a brainstorm. Start with the places where customers already tell you what confuses them:

  • Support tickets and chat transcripts. Export the last few months and tag each conversation by topic.
  • Site search with no results. These queries show what people expected to find and didn't.
  • Sales and onboarding calls. Prospects ask about pricing, setup, and limits long before they file a ticket.
  • Feedback on existing help articles. A "not helpful" vote on a page often points to a missing question.

Count how often each topic appears. Your FAQ page should cover the top of that list, not every question ever asked.

Keep the customer's wording. The Nielsen Norman Group points out that your vocabulary and your users' vocabulary often "talk past each other." If customers ask how to "cancel my plan," don't title the question "Subscription termination."

If your documentation has an AI assistant, its unanswered questions are a rich source too. In Sonat, AI Insights records every question the AI couldn't answer and ranks the gaps by how often they occur, so you can see demand for content that doesn't exist yet.

Step 2: Decide what belongs on the FAQ page (and what doesn't)

Before adding a question to your FAQ section, ask: does this answer already live somewhere else?

If the question is…Put the answer…
Short, factual, and asked often ("Do you offer refunds?")On the FAQ page
A multi-step task ("How do I connect my calendar?")In a full how-to article, and link to it from the FAQ
Already covered by an existing help articleIn that article, and fix the article so people can find it
Asked often because a page is confusingIn a rewrite of the confusing page

A good rule: the FAQ page answers the question in a few sentences, and links to the full help article for everything else. It's a fast lane into your help center, not a second copy of it.

Step 3: Write answers people can act on

Each answer should stand alone, because people often land on a single question from search or a support reply.

Lead with the answer. If the question is "Can I change my plan mid-month?", start with "Yes" or "No," then explain.

Front-load the key words. Phrase questions the way customers search, with the important words first. "Refunds: can I get my money back?" scans faster than "What is your company's policy regarding the issuance of refunds?"

Keep it short. Two to four sentences is enough for most answers. If you need more, the answer probably belongs in a full article.

Write plainly. Use "you," active verbs, and everyday words. The Nielsen Norman Group recommends answers that are factual and frank, not marketing copy.

Link onward. End longer answers with a link to the full instructions, so the reader's next step is one click away.

Here's a simple FAQ template you can reuse for each entry:

Question (in the customer's words): Can I invite people outside my company?

Short answer: Yes. Workspace admins can invite external guests from the Members page.

Detail (optional, 1–2 sentences): Guests can view and comment, but they can't edit.

Next step: Link to the full "Invite external guests" article.

Step 4: Structure the page so people can scan it

Most visitors don't read an FAQ page top to bottom. They scan for their question and read only that answer.

Help them do that:

  • Group questions by topic. Billing, account, setup, troubleshooting. Large FAQs should be "chunked by topic," as the Nielsen Norman Group puts it, so readers can skip whole sections at a glance.
  • Order by demand. Put the most-asked question in each group first.
  • Make questions visually distinct. Use clear headings so questions stand out from answers.
  • Give each question its own anchor link. Support agents can then reply with a link that jumps straight to the answer.
  • Keep it mobile-friendly. Collapsible sections save space on small screens, but make sure the page is still searchable with the browser's find function.

If your FAQ grows past a few dozen questions, some topics have probably outgrown it and deserve their own help articles.

Step 5: Put the FAQ page where people need help

An FAQ page only deflects tickets if people see it before they reach out.

  • Link it from your contact or support page, above the contact form, not below it.
  • Surface answers in context. A help widget in your product can show the relevant answer on the screen where the question comes up. Sonat's help widget, for example, links each topic to its matching knowledge base section.
  • Let customers ask in their own words. An AI assistant trained on your documentation, like Sonat's AI Answer Generator, can answer questions from your manuals and FAQs, even when the wording doesn't match your headings.
  • Use FAQ links in support replies. Customers learn where to look next time.

This is the core of case deflection: guiding customers to an answer through self-service so they don't need an agent.

Step 6: Keep your FAQ page current

An outdated FAQ is worse than none, because it gives confident, wrong answers. Treat the page as a living document:

  • Review it on a set schedule, such as every quarter, and whenever your product, pricing, or policies change.
  • Retire stale questions. If nobody asks something anymore, remove it.
  • Assign an owner. Someone should be responsible for each topic group.
  • Track a few simple signals: tickets on FAQ topics, failed searches, and "not helpful" votes. If tickets on a topic don't drop after you add an answer, rewrite the answer.

Version history helps: when you can restore an earlier version, regular edits feel safe.

FAQ page best practices: a quick checklist

Before you publish, check that your FAQ page:

  • ☐ Uses questions from real tickets, searches, and calls, in customers' own words
  • ☐ Answers each question in a few sentences, with the answer first
  • ☐ Links to full help articles for multi-step tasks
  • ☐ Groups questions by topic, with the most-asked first
  • ☐ Gives each question its own linkable anchor
  • ☐ Is linked from your contact page and support replies
  • ☐ Has an owner and a review date

Conclusion

A good FAQ page isn't a list of guesses. It's a short, scannable summary of what customers really ask, built from support data and connected to the rest of your help center. Start with your tickets, keep answers short, link onward, and review often.
