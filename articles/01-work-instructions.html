Work Instructions: How to Write Them So People Actually Follow Them

A new team member is standing at a workstation, a service desk, or a packing line. They have read the policy and skimmed the procedure, and they still don't know which button to press first. Work instructions exist for that moment.

This guide explains what work instructions are, how they differ from SOPs and procedures, and how to write them in a format people can follow on their first try. It also covers how to keep them accurate after the process changes.

What is a work instruction?

A work instruction is a step-by-step document that tells one person how to complete one specific task. It covers the exact actions, in order, plus the details that make each step succeed or stay safe. A policy says why, a procedure says what and who, and a work instruction says how.

A procedure might say "Quality inspects incoming parts before they are stored." The work instruction for that procedure explains how to do the inspection: which gauge to use, where to measure, what counts as a pass, and what to do with a reject.

Work instructions vs SOPs and procedures

Teams often mix these terms up, and the result is documents that are either too vague to follow or too detailed to maintain. In quality management, a common way to organize documentation is a hierarchy:

  1. Policy. Your commitments and rules (the why).
  2. Manual. How your overall system fits together.
  3. Procedures and SOPs. Which activities happen, in what order, and who owns them (the what and who).
  4. Work instructions. How one person performs one task (the how).
  5. Records and forms. Proof that the work was done.

Quality frameworks such as ISO 9001 treat this structure as a convention, not a requirement. You can decide how big each document is. A work instruction can sit inside a procedure or be referenced from it. In practice, though, separating them pays off. Procedures change when responsibilities change, while work instructions change when the task itself changes, such as a new tool, a new screen, or a new supplier part.

Detail should match the reader. Well-trained, experienced staff need less detail than new starters. If your team turns over often, or if a task is safety-critical or done rarely, write more detail rather than less.

How to write work instructions: a 6-step method

The most reliable method for writing work instructions dates back to the 1940s. The U.S. Training Within Industry (TWI) program's Job Instruction method breaks a job into important steps, key points, and reasons. Lean manufacturing still uses it today. You don't need a factory to use it. It works just as well for configuring a laptop or processing a refund.

1. Pick one task and one reader

Name the task as an outcome ("Replace the filter cartridge on Line 2," "Issue a partial refund"). Then name who will use it. One work instruction should serve one role doing one task. If you keep writing "if you are in finance, do X; if you are in support, do Y," you have two work instructions.

2. Watch the task before you write it

Write alongside the person who does the job best, not from memory. Experts skip steps without noticing. Ask them to do the task slowly and narrate it. Capture where they look, what they check, and what they do when something goes wrong.

3. List the important steps

An important step is a main stage that moves the job forward, not every small movement. Start each step with a verb: Remove, Check, Enter, Tighten. Keep a step to one action where you can.

Aim for a short list. People hold only a few chunks of information in short-term memory at once, so a long task is easier to follow when you group it into short sections with their own headings.

4. Add the key points

Key points are what separate doing a step from doing it right. They capture the special knack needed to do the job safely, with good quality, and efficiently. Look for:

  • Safety. In TWI, safety is always a key point.
  • Sensory cues. How the part or tool should feel, the sound it makes, or its temperature.
  • Special motions or information. The critical setting, the exact order, or the detail that experts know and newcomers miss.

For example, the step "Tighten the cap" has the key point "Hand-tight plus a quarter turn. Over-tightening cracks the seal."

5. Explain the reason for each key point

People skip rules they don't understand, especially under time pressure. One short "why" per key point turns an instruction into understanding: "because the seal cracks if over-tightened, and a cracked seal leaks under pressure." The reason also helps people handle situations the instruction didn't anticipate.

6. Test it with someone new

Hand the draft to someone who has never done the task. Watch them follow it without help. Every time they hesitate, ask a question, or go wrong, you've found a gap in the document. Fix it and test again.

A simple work instructions format

A consistent format means readers always know where to look. This structure works for most tasks:

  • Title: the task as an outcome
  • Purpose: one sentence on why the task matters
  • Scope and role: who performs it, and when
  • Before you start: tools, materials, access, and safety equipment
  • Steps: numbered, one action each, with key points and reasons
  • Checks: how to confirm the result is correct
  • If something goes wrong: common problems and who to contact
  • Owner and version: who maintains the instruction and which version this is

Write the steps in plain language. Use active voice and address the reader directly: "Check the pressure gauge" instead of "The pressure gauge should be checked." Active voice makes clear who does what. Use the present tense and the simplest verb available ("check," not "perform a check of").

Work instructions example

Here is a short, illustrative work instruction written in this format. It shows the structure; for real equipment, always follow the manufacturer's instructions.

Title: Replace the filter cartridge on a water dispenser

Purpose: A clogged filter slows flow and affects water quality.

Role: Facilities technician, every 6 months or when the filter light turns red.

Before you start: New filter cartridge, towel, gloves.

  1. Switch off the water supply valve under the unit.
    • Key point: Turn it fully clockwise. Reason: A partly open valve sprays water when the filter is removed.
  2. Twist the old cartridge a quarter turn left and pull it down.
    • Key point: Hold the towel underneath. Reason: About a cup of water drains out.
  3. Remove the cap from the new cartridge and push it up into the housing.
    • Key point: Line up the arrow on the cartridge with the arrow on the housing. Reason: A misaligned cartridge won't seal.
  4. Twist the new cartridge a quarter turn right until it clicks.
  5. Open the supply valve and run water for 2 minutes.
    • Key point: Discard this water. Reason: It flushes carbon dust from the new filter.

Check: The flow is steady and the filter light is green.

If something goes wrong: If water leaks at the housing, close the valve, remove the cartridge, and repeat step 3.

Owner: Facilities lead · Version 3

This example has five steps, each with one action and a reason for every key point. That is enough for a newcomer to do the job safely the first time.

Visual work instructions: when to show, not tell

Some steps are faster to show than to describe: where a part goes, which way a connector faces, or which field to click. Visual work instructions add photos, annotated screenshots, diagrams, or short videos next to the text.

A few rules keep visuals useful:

  • One image per step, placed beside that step. An image gallery at the end forces the reader to match pictures to steps.
  • Highlight what matters. Circle the valve, arrow the button, crop away clutter.
  • Keep the text. Visuals support the steps; they don't replace them. Text is searchable, translatable, and accessible to screen readers.
  • Re-shoot when the equipment or screen changes. An outdated photo is more confusing than none.

Keep work instructions current

A work instruction is only useful if it matches how the work is done today. Lean practice treats standardized work as a baseline for improvement. You review and update the standard as conditions change, so every improvement becomes the new normal.

That means work instructions need the same controls as the rest of your policies and procedures:

  • One source of truth. Publish instructions in one place that everyone can reach on a phone, tablet, or shop-floor screen, not in printouts and shared drives.
  • Version history. Readers should see the current version, and you should be able to see what changed and when.
  • Review and approval. A subject-matter expert checks every change before it goes live.
  • Feedback from the floor. The people doing the work spot outdated steps first. Give them an easy way to flag problems on the instruction itself.
  • Translations. If your team works in several languages, every reader needs the same current version in their language.

Conclusion

Good work instructions are not longer documents. They are more specific ones. Keep each to one task and one reader. Break it into important steps, add the key points that make each step work, and explain why. Then test it with someone new, add visuals where words fall short, and keep it updated as the work changes.

Start with the task that causes the most questions or errors on your team. Write it once, properly, and you'll see the difference in the next new hire's first week.
