Work Instructions: A Practical Guide to Writing Them Well

Your policies say what must happen. Your procedures say who does it and when. But the person at the workstation, the help desk, or the loading dock needs something more specific: exactly how to do this one task, step by step, right now.

That is the job of a work instruction. This guide covers what work instructions are, how they differ from SOPs, what to put in them, and how to keep them accurate once they are in use.

What is a work instruction?

A work instruction is a short, step-by-step document that explains how to complete one specific task. It sits below a procedure: the procedure describes the overall process and who is responsible, while the work instruction shows one person exactly how to carry out a single part of it, in order, with the tools and checks involved.

When a team agrees on one approved way to do a task and everyone follows it, these are often called standard work instructions. A procedure might say "New laptops are configured by IT before handover." The work instruction for that step tells the technician which settings to apply, in which order, and how to confirm each one worked.

Work instructions vs SOPs: what's the difference?

People often use "SOP" and "work instruction" interchangeably, and some organizations do fold both into one document. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's guidance on preparing SOPs even notes that terms like protocols, instructions, and worksheets are used for the same kind of document. Still, separating the two makes each one easier to write and easier to use.

The practical difference is scope and level of detail:

Standard operating procedure (SOP)Work instruction
CoversA whole process, often across rolesOne task, usually done by one role
AnswersWhat happens, who is responsible, whenExactly how to do it, step by step
Typical readerManagers, process owners, auditorsThe person doing the task
DetailModerate; points to supporting documentsHigh; every action, setting, and check
ChangesLess oftenMore often, as tools and methods change

A useful rule: if a step in your SOP needs more than a sentence or two to explain, it probably deserves its own work instruction. Link to it from the SOP instead of stuffing the detail into the procedure. If you are still building the procedure layer, start with our guide to standard operating procedures.

Where work instructions fit in your documentation

Most teams that run a quality or compliance program organize their documents in layers:

  1. Policies — the rules and the reasons for them.
  2. Procedures (SOPs) — the process that puts a policy into practice.
  3. Work instructions — how to do each task within that process.
  4. Forms, checklists, and records — what gets filled in and kept as evidence.

The layers only work if they are connected: every work instruction should point to its parent procedure, and every procedure should link to the instructions behind it.

What to include: a simple work instruction format

There is no single mandatory work instruction template, but good ones share the same building blocks. The EPA's SOP guidance recommends starting with the purpose and scope, defining specialized terms, and calling out equipment, personnel qualifications, and safety considerations before the steps. That structure translates directly to work instructions:

  • Title and ID — a short, specific name ("Calibrate the line 2 label printer") and a document number.
  • Purpose — one or two sentences on why the task matters.
  • Scope — which equipment, location, product, or system this applies to, and what it does not cover.
  • Who performs it — the role, plus any training or certification required.
  • Tools and materials — everything to gather before starting.
  • Safety and warnings — hazards and required protective equipment, placed before the steps and repeated at the step where they apply.
  • Steps — numbered, in order, one action per step.
  • Checks — how the person knows each critical step, and the whole task, was done correctly.
  • References — the parent procedure and any related forms or checklists.
  • Revision details — version number, date, owner, and approver.

The EPA guidance recommends putting the ID, revision number, and date on each page, because it lets anyone confirm they are reading the current version. In a web-based manual, that information belongs at the top of the page.

How to write work instructions people actually follow

1. Write with the people who do the work

The best source for a work instruction is the person who performs the task every day. OSHA's guidance on job hazard analysis stresses involving employees in breaking a job into steps, and reviewing those steps with them to make sure nothing was left out. The EPA guidance makes the same point about procedures: they should be written by people who actually perform the work, and a team approach builds buy-in from the people who will use them.

OSHA also suggests photographing or filming a worker performing the job. That works just as well for documentation. Record the task once, then write from the recording instead of from memory.

2. Get the level of detail right

OSHA's advice on breaking a job into steps applies directly here: avoid a breakdown so detailed that it becomes unnecessarily long, or so broad that it skips basic steps. A good test is to ask whether a trained but new team member could complete each step without asking a question. If they could not, the step is too broad. If a step is something no one would ever get wrong ("Walk to the machine"), cut it.

3. Use short, direct, numbered steps

The EPA guidance asks for a concise, step-by-step, easy-to-read format, using the active voice and present tense, and avoiding wordy, redundant, or overly long text. In practice:

  • Start each step with a verb: "Open," "Select," "Tighten," "Confirm."
  • Put one action in each step.
  • Put warnings before the step they apply to, not after.
  • Name buttons, fields, and parts exactly as they appear on screen or on the equipment.

Number your steps. The U.S. government's plain language guidance on lists recommends numbering items when you outline steps in a process, keeping list items parallel in structure, and not overusing lists for trivial points. For more on sentence-level craft, see our guide on how to write step-by-step instructions.

4. Show, don't just tell

A clear photo, annotated screenshot, or short video often replaces a paragraph of text. Visual work instructions are especially helpful when the task involves physical parts or a screen with many options. Add an image where a reader is most likely to hesitate, such as identifying the right component or the right setting. The EPA guidance also suggests using a flowchart to illustrate the process being described.

5. Test the draft with someone who didn't write it

Authors fill gaps from memory without noticing. The EPA guidance notes it is especially helpful when drafts are tested by someone other than the original writer. Hand the draft to a person who knows the role but not this task, watch them follow it, and note every pause or question. Each one is a missing or unclear step.

6. Review, approve, and publish one current version

The EPA guidance describes review by someone experienced with the process, followed by approval from a supervisor and the quality lead, and it accepts electronic signatures in place of paper. Then publish one current version where people work. The same guidance warns that instructions only help if current copies are readily available in the work area, and notes that read-only electronic access protects them from unauthorized changes.

A short work instructions example

Here is a compact example for an office IT task, using the format above.

Title: Set up a new employee laptop (WI-IT-014, revision 3)

Purpose: Make sure every new laptop is secure and ready on the employee's first day.

Scope: Company-issued laptops for new hires. Does not cover loaner devices.

Performed by: IT support technician.

Tools: Laptop, charger, new-hire ticket, asset tag label.

  1. Open the new-hire ticket and confirm the employee's name, team, and start date.
  2. Attach the asset tag to the underside of the laptop.
  3. Record the asset tag number and serial number in the ticket.
  4. Power on the laptop and connect it to the office network.
  5. Sign in with the setup account listed in the ticket.
  6. Apply the standard device settings for the employee's team.
  7. Install pending system updates and restart.
  8. Check: Confirm the device shows as compliant in the device management console.
  9. Sign out, close the lid, and place the laptop with its charger in the handover bin.
  10. Update the ticket status to "Ready for handover."

Parent procedure: Employee onboarding (SOP-HR-002).

Notice what is missing: no policy background and no explanation of why the company manages devices. That belongs in the policy and the procedure.

Keep work instructions current

Work instructions go out of date faster than any other document layer, because tools, screens, and equipment change. Build upkeep into the process:

  • Review on a schedule. The EPA guidance suggests reviewing procedures periodically, for example every one to two years, and adding the review date to each one. High-change tasks may need a shorter cycle.
  • Review after incidents and changes. OSHA notes it is particularly important to revisit a job analysis after an injury or illness on that job, and to train affected employees whenever methods or procedures change.
  • Retire what is no longer used. If a task is no longer performed that way, withdraw the instruction and archive it so no one follows an old version.
  • Collect feedback from readers. The people following the instruction will spot problems first. Make it easy for them to say so.

Conclusion

A work instruction does one thing: it shows one person how to complete one task correctly, every time. Keep it narrow, write it with the people who do the work, test it on someone new, and publish a single current version where the work happens.
