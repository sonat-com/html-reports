IT Runbooks: How to Write Runbooks Your Team Actually Uses

It's 2 a.m. A payment service is throwing errors, an alert is paging whoever is on call, and the one engineer who knows how to restart it cleanly is on vacation. The person staring at the screen has two choices: improvise under pressure, or open a document that tells them exactly what to do next. That document is a runbook — and whether it exists, and whether anyone trusts it, is the difference between a five-minute fix and a two-hour outage.

This guide covers what a runbook is, how it differs from a playbook, what to put in one, and how to keep it accurate enough that your team reaches for it instead of guessing.

What is a runbook?

A runbook is a written, step-by-step procedure for carrying out a specific, repeatable operational task — restarting a service, rotating a database, clearing a queue, or recovering from a known failure. It exists so that any qualified person, not just the author, can complete the task correctly and safely.

Runbooks are not new. Operations teams have kept them since the mainframe era; a 1978 industry account already noted that operators "need and want a runbook for each application." What has changed is the format — from binders next to the server to living documents linked directly from the alerts that trigger them.

The value is speed and consistency. When on-call engineers follow documented steps instead of reconstructing them from memory, they reduce mean time to repair, cut the risk of human error, and lower the stress of responding to an incident at 2 a.m. A good runbook turns a scramble into a checklist.

Runbook vs. playbook: what's the difference?

The two terms are often used interchangeably, and in day-to-day conversation that's fine. But the distinction is useful when you're deciding what to write.

  • A runbook is tactical. It answers how do I perform this one procedure — the exact commands, the order, the checks. It assumes you already know which failure you're dealing with.
  • A playbook is strategic. It answers how do we respond to this category of situation — who takes which role, how we communicate, when we escalate, and which decisions need a human.

Put simply: a playbook coordinates the response; a runbook executes a step within it. A major-incident playbook might say "restore the primary region," and link to the specific runbook that restores it. You need both. Start with runbooks for your most common, most repeatable tasks, because those are where documented steps pay off fastest — then layer playbooks on top for the messier, judgment-heavy scenarios.

What goes into a good runbook

The best runbooks are short, scannable, and specific. Every step should be an instruction you can act on, not a paragraph you have to interpret. A reliable template covers the same fields every time so readers always know where to look.

  • Title and purpose — what this runbook does, in one line ("Restart the payments API").
  • When to use it — the trigger, symptom, or alert that sends someone here.
  • Owner and last-reviewed date — who maintains it and when it was last checked.
  • Prerequisites — access, permissions, and tools needed before starting.
  • Severity and impact — how urgent this is and what it affects, so the reader can prioritize.
  • Steps — numbered, ordered actions with the exact commands or clicks, and the expected result after each one.
  • Verification — how to confirm the task actually worked.
  • Rollback — how to undo the change if it makes things worse.
  • Escalation — who to contact, and when, if the steps don't resolve it.

If a step could fail in a predictable way, say so and give the recovery path. The goal is that a competent teammate who has never done this task can follow it end to end without a phone call.

How to write a runbook, step by step

You don't need a documentation project to start. You need one procedure and thirty focused minutes.

  1. Pick a real, frequent task. List the five things your team does most often during incidents or routine operations. Start with the one that's done most and documented least.
  2. Do the task and narrate it. Perform the procedure — or shadow someone who does — and record every command, screen, and decision as it happens. Live capture beats memory.
  3. Write each step as an action plus an expected result. "Run systemctl restart payments. The service returns to active (running) within 30 seconds." Concrete beats vague every time.
  4. Add the guardrails. Fill in verification, rollback, and escalation. These are what let a nervous reader trust the document at 2 a.m.
  5. Have someone else run it cold. The real test is a teammate following the runbook without help. Wherever they hesitate or ask a question, the runbook has a gap. Fix it and move on.

Then link the finished runbook directly from the alert or ticket that should trigger it, so nobody has to hunt for it mid-incident. A runbook no one can find is the same as no runbook.

From manual to automated runbooks

Runbooks live on a spectrum, and most teams move along it over time.

  • Manual — a human reads the steps and performs each one. This is where every runbook should start, because writing it forces you to understand and standardize the procedure.
  • Semi-automated — scripts handle the mechanical parts while a person makes the judgment calls and approves each stage. The runbook becomes a checklist wrapped around automation.
  • Automated — well-understood, low-risk procedures run on their own, with the runbook as the source of record and audit trail.

The important part is the order. Automating a procedure you haven't first documented and stabilized just makes mistakes faster. Write the manual runbook, prove it works by having people follow it, and automate only the steps that are truly routine and safe. Every automation should still point back to a readable runbook that explains what it does and how to intervene.

Keeping runbooks accurate — the hard part

Writing a runbook is easy. Keeping it true is where most documentation quietly dies. Systems change, commands drift, and a runbook that was right last quarter can send someone down the wrong path today — which is worse than no runbook, because it's trusted.

A few habits keep runbooks honest:

  • Give every runbook an owner and a review date. Documentation is a standing responsibility, not a one-time task. Unowned docs rot.
  • Update the runbook as part of the change. When a procedure changes, the runbook edit is part of the work, not a follow-up someone forgets.
  • Keep one source of truth. Scattered copies in chat threads, personal notes, and old wikis guarantee that people follow the wrong version. Everyone should read from the same place.
  • Keep the version history. When a step changes, you want to see what it was, who changed it, and when — both to review changes and to recover a step that was removed by mistake.
  • Review before it goes live. For anything high-impact, a second set of eyes and an approval step catches errors before an on-call engineer runs them for real.

Start with one

You don't need a perfect documentation system to get value from runbooks. You need one good one, for the task your team dreads doing under pressure. Write it, have a teammate run it cold, link it from the alert that triggers it, and give it an owner who keeps it honest. Then write the next one. A small library of runbooks your team actually trusts will do more for your uptime — and your on-call sleep — than any amount of documentation nobody reads.
