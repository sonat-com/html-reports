How to Create a Documentation Style Guide Your Team Will Use

Five people write your help docs. One says "log in," another says "sign in," and a third writes "Click the Save button" while everyone else writes "Select Save." No single page is wrong. But together, the docs read like they came from five different companies, and readers notice.

A documentation style guide fixes this. This guide shows you how to build one in six steps. The result is short, practical, and easy to keep up to date, whether your writers are full-time technical writers, support agents, or AI tools that draft your first versions.

What is a documentation style guide?

A documentation style guide is a shared set of rules for how your team writes and formats docs: voice and tone, word choices, grammar, formatting, and page structure. It gives every author one place to check how to write something, so your docs read as if one person wrote them.

It can start very small. The Write the Docs community, a vendor-neutral group of documentation professionals, points out that a style guide can be as simple as a list of decisions you've made about how to refer to things.

Why consistency matters to readers

Consistency isn't about being tidy. It helps the reader.

  • Less mental effort. When the same thing always has the same name, readers don't stop to wonder if "workspace" and "project" mean different things.
  • More trust. Consistent docs feel more authoritative. Inconsistent ones make readers question the content itself.
  • Faster scanning. Most people don't read help pages word for word. In a classic Nielsen Norman Group study, 79% of test users always scanned new web pages, and only 16% read word by word. Predictable headings, lists, and formatting are what make scanning work.

There's a team benefit too. A style guide ends repeat debates. Once you decide "sign in" beats "log in," nobody has to argue about it in a review again.

Step 1: Start from an existing style guide

You don't need to write every rule yourself. Pick a published, general-purpose style guide as your base. Several large organizations, including government digital teams, publish theirs for free. Then write down only the places where your team does things differently.

That way, your own guide covers:

  1. Your base guide. "For anything not covered here, follow [base guide]."
  2. Your exceptions. The rules where you go a different way.
  3. Your additions. Product names, audience details, and templates no general guide could know.

This keeps your document short. A short guide gets read. A 60-page guide gets bookmarked and forgotten.

Step 2: Define your voice and tone guidelines

Voice is your docs' personality, and it stays the same everywhere. Tone changes with the situation. A setup guide can be warm and upbeat, but an error message about lost data should be calm and direct.

Nielsen Norman Group describes tone along four dimensions:

DimensionOne endOther end
FormalityFormalCasual
HumorSeriousFunny
RespectRespectfulIrreverent
EnthusiasmMatter-of-factEnthusiastic

For each dimension, mark where your docs belong. Most product documentation sits near respectful, matter-of-fact, and serious, with formality depending on your audience. Then add one or two "write this, not that" pairs:

  • Write: "Your changes weren't saved. Try again in a few minutes."
  • Not: "Oops! Something went wrong!"

Real before-and-after examples teach new writers more than adjectives like "friendly" or "professional" ever will.

Step 3: Build a word list for your terminology

The word list is one of the most practical parts of a technical writing style guide. It's an A to Z list of terms with the approved spelling, capitalization, and usage. The UK government's public style guide, for example, is organized as an A to Z because it's fast to search.

Start with the words that cause the most trouble:

  • Product and feature names: exact spelling and capitalization.
  • Competing synonyms: pick one and list the ones to avoid. For example, sign in (not log in or login as a verb).
  • UI actions: for example, select rather than click if your docs are read on touch screens.
  • Jargon: terms to avoid, or to define on first use.
  • Audience words: customer, user, member, or admin? Choose one and stick to it.

Don't try to finish the list on day one. Add an entry every time a reviewer catches the same issue twice.

Step 4: Set formatting and structure rules

Formatting rules are what make docs scannable. Keep this section to the decisions your writers actually face every week:

  • Headings: sentence case or title case. Pick one.
  • Lists: numbered lists for steps in order, bullets for everything else.
  • UI elements: put button and menu names in bold, for example Settings > Users.
  • Conditions first: "If you're an admin, select Invite," not "Select Invite if you're an admin." Readers need the condition before they act.
  • Voice and person: active voice, and "you" for the reader.
  • Images: when to use a screenshot, and alt text on every image.
  • Numbers and dates: a date format nobody can misread, such as "October 17, 2026" rather than "10/17."

Step 5: Turn your rules into templates

Rules tell writers what to do. Templates make it easy. Create a starting page for each common type of doc:

  • How-to guide: goal, prerequisites, numbered steps, expected result.
  • Concept page: what it is, why it matters, how it relates to other features.
  • Troubleshooting article: symptom, cause, fix.
  • Release notes: what changed, who it affects, what to do.

Each template should already follow your style rules, with the headings in the right case and sample text in the right voice. A writer who starts from a template gets most of the style guide right without opening it. If your docs tool supports templates, as Sonat does, store them there so they're one click away when someone creates a new topic.

Step 6: Write a style guide your AI tools can follow

If your team uses AI tools to write first drafts that people then review and edit, your style guide becomes more important, not less. AI tools follow the instructions you give them, so put your style guide into those instructions.

To make your guide work for AI tools:

  • Write rules as clear instructions. "Use 'sign in,' never 'log in'" works better than "We generally prefer 'sign in.'"
  • Include examples. The "write this, not that" pairs from Step 2 show the model the tone you want.
  • Add your word list. It's the fastest way to stop the AI from mixing up your product terms.
  • Keep human review. AI drafts can sound polished but still get product details wrong, mix up terms, or make claims nobody can back up. Your reviewers check facts and accuracy. The style guide covers everything else.

Here's a useful side effect. Rules clear enough for an AI tool to follow are clear enough for a new hire, too.

How to keep your style guide alive

A style guide only helps if people use it. These habits keep it current:

  • Name an owner. One person approves changes, so the guide doesn't fill up with contradictions.
  • Put it where writers work. Link it from your templates and your review checklist.
  • Use reviews to spot gaps. If the same comment shows up three times, turn it into a rule.
  • Date your changes. A short changelog shows writers what's new.
  • Automate the boring parts. Readability checks catch long sentences and passive voice before a reviewer has to. Sonat includes a built-in readability guide for this, and approval workflows make sure every topic gets a second look before it's published.

Start small and grow it

You don't need a perfect documentation style guide to get started. You need a base guide to point to, a short list of your own exceptions, and a word list that grows every week. Begin with the five rules your reviewers repeat most often, write them down this week, and build from there.
