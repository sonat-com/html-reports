How to Write a Standard Operating Procedure People Can Actually Follow

Most teams have a process that lives in one person's head. It works fine until that person is on vacation, changes roles, or simply forgets a step. A standard operating procedure (SOP) fixes that by putting the process on paper so anyone can repeat it the same way every time.

This guide shows you how to write a standard operating procedure from scratch. It covers what to document, how to phrase each step, how to test and approve the draft, and how to keep it current. At the end you'll find a copy-ready SOP template.

What is a standard operating procedure?

A standard operating procedure is a set of written instructions for a routine or repetitive activity that an organization carries out. Its job is to make sure the task is done correctly and consistently, whoever does it.

SOPs are not only for labs and factories. The same approach works for administrative work, such as onboarding a new hire, approving a vendor invoice, handling a data access request, or publishing a policy update.

SOP vs. policy: what's the difference?

People often mix these up, so it helps to separate them before you start writing:

  • A policy states what the organization requires and why. For example: "All customer refunds over $500 need manager approval."
  • An SOP explains how to carry out that requirement, step by step. For example: open the refund request, check the amount, route it to the manager, and record the decision.

A policy rarely changes. An SOP changes whenever the tools or the workflow change. Keep them as separate documents and link the SOP back to the policy it supports.

Why SOPs are worth the effort

A well-written SOP:

  • Reduces variation. Everyone follows the same steps, even when people join or leave the team.
  • Speeds up training. New staff learn from detailed, written instructions instead of shadowing someone for weeks.
  • Supports compliance and audits. Auditors and inspectors often use SOPs as checklists when they review how work is done.
  • Prevents miscommunication. Clear steps leave less room for "I thought you meant…"

One warning: even the best SOP fails if no one follows it, or if people can't find the current version. Writing it is only half the job. The other half is making it easy to reach and keeping it up to date.

How to write a standard operating procedure in 8 steps

1. Decide which processes need an SOP

Not every task needs a formal procedure. Prioritize processes that are:

  • Repeated often, by more than one person
  • Critical to safety, quality, or compliance
  • Error-prone, or the source of frequent questions
  • Rarely performed, so people forget the details between runs

The level of detail can vary too. A critical process that many people perform needs more detail than a low-risk task done by one experienced person.

2. Involve the people who do the work

SOPs should be written by people who know the activity firsthand: the subject-matter experts who actually perform it. A manager describing a process from memory will miss the workarounds and edge cases that happen in practice.

For processes that span several roles, write as a small team. You'll capture more of the real workflow, and the people who helped write the SOP are more likely to follow it.

3. Define the purpose, scope, and roles

Before listing any steps, answer three questions at the top of the document:

  • Purpose: Why does this procedure exist? What outcome should it produce?
  • Scope: When does it apply, and when doesn't it? Which teams, locations, or systems does it cover?
  • Roles and responsibilities: Who performs each part, who approves it, and what training or access do they need first?

Define any acronyms or specialized terms here too, so readers don't have to guess.

4. Choose the right SOP format

There is no single correct SOP format. Pick the one that matches how the process actually flows:

  • Simple numbered steps. This is best for short, linear tasks with no decisions along the way.
  • Hierarchical steps. Use numbered steps with sub-steps when individual steps carry detail that would clutter a flat list.
  • Flowchart. This is best when the process branches, for example when the next action depends on an approval, a check, or a customer's answer.

Many good SOPs combine formats: a short flowchart for the overview, followed by numbered steps for the detail.

5. Write each step in plain language

This is where most SOPs succeed or fail. Write for someone who has a basic understanding of the work but has never done this particular task, and who will follow your instructions without supervision.

Follow these plain-language rules:

  • Start each step with a verb. Use the imperative mood: "Open the request form," not "The request form should be opened."
  • Use the active voice and present tense. The active voice makes it clear who does what.
  • Put one action in each step. If a step contains "and then," split it in two.
  • Prefer simple words. Write "use," not "utilize"; "check," not "perform a verification of."
  • Be specific. Name the exact system, field, button, or form. Replace "update the record" with "In the CRM, set Status to Approved."
  • Flag warnings where they matter. Put cautions in bold at the step where the risk occurs, not only in an introduction nobody rereads.
  • Use lists and visuals. Lists add white space and reduce ambiguity. Screenshots, diagrams, and flowcharts break up long text.

Here's a before-and-after example:

Before: The invoice should be checked against the purchase order and, if everything is in order, it can be forwarded for approval.

After:

  1. Open the invoice in the finance inbox.
  2. Compare the amount and line items with the matching purchase order.
  3. If they match, forward the invoice to the budget owner for approval.
  4. If they don't match, return the invoice to the vendor with a note explaining the difference.

6. Test the draft with someone else

Before finalizing, ask a colleague who didn't write the SOP to perform the task using only the draft. Watch where they hesitate, ask questions, or go off-script. Each of those moments marks a step that needs rewriting.

Then have one or more people with experience of the process review it for accuracy.

7. Get it approved and put it under document control

Once the content is right, route the SOP through your approval process. Typically that means the immediate supervisor plus whoever owns quality or compliance. Their sign-off confirms that management has reviewed and approved it. Electronic approvals work just as well as paper signatures, as long as they are recorded.

Give every SOP basic document-control details:

  • A short title and a unique ID number
  • A revision number and effective date
  • The owner and the approvers

These details tell readers they're looking at the current version. They also make it possible to reconstruct which version was in use on a given date during an audit or investigation.

8. Publish it where people work, then review it regularly

An SOP locked in a shared drive folder nobody opens serves little purpose. Make the current version easy to reach from wherever the work happens, whether that's a help center, an internal knowledge base, or a link inside the tool people use.

Then schedule reviews. A common rule of thumb is to review each SOP every one to two years, and immediately whenever the process, system, or underlying policy changes. Record the review date on the SOP. If a procedure is no longer followed, withdraw it and archive it rather than leaving it to confuse people.

What about checklists?

Checklists are useful for confirming that steps happen in order and for recording that they're done. But the checklist is not the SOP. It's one part of it. Reference the checklist at the step where it's used, and keep the full explanation in the SOP itself.

Standard operating procedure template

Copy this outline as a starting point and adapt it to your process. Small, low-risk procedures can drop sections that don't apply.

Header

  • SOP title
  • SOP ID and revision number
  • Effective date and next review date
  • Owner and approvers

1. Purpose. One or two sentences on why this procedure exists and the outcome it produces.

2. Scope. When the procedure applies, and when it doesn't. Teams, locations, or systems covered.

3. Related policy. The policy or requirement this SOP puts into practice.

4. Definitions. Acronyms and specialized terms used in the document.

5. Roles and responsibilities. Who performs, approves, and reviews each part, and any required training or access.

6. Tools and materials. Systems, forms, templates, and equipment needed.

7. Safety notes and cautions. Risks to people, data, or equipment. Repeat each one at the step where it applies.

8. Procedure. Numbered steps, one action each, starting with a verb. Add sub-steps, screenshots, or a flowchart where needed.

9. Quality checks. How the person confirms the task was done correctly, and what to do if it wasn't.

10. Records. What to record, which forms to use, and where records are stored.

11. Revision history. Date, revision number, summary of changes, and approver for each version.

Keep your SOPs alive, not archived

Writing a standard operating procedure comes down to a few habits: involve the people who do the work, write one clear action per step, test the draft with someone new, and keep the approved version easy to find and up to date.
