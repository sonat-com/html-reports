How to Write Technical Documentation: A 7-Step Process

You know your product inside out. Your readers don't. That gap is why good technical documentation is hard to write, and why it is worth getting right. Clear docs answer questions before they become support tickets and save your team from explaining the same thing over and over.

This guide walks through how to write technical documentation in seven practical steps, from knowing your reader to keeping the docs current. You don't need to be a professional technical writer to follow it. You need a clear process.

In short: to write technical documentation, define who the reader is and what they need to do, choose the right type of document, outline it as small single-purpose topics, draft for scanning in plain language, review with experts and real users, publish where people will look, and update it every time the product changes.

What is technical documentation?

Technical documentation is any content that helps people understand, use, set up, or fix a product or process. That covers user manuals, help center articles, setup guides, troubleshooting pages, internal runbooks, and reference material such as settings or specifications.

Each one serves a reader with a goal: finish a task, understand a concept, or look up a fact. Every step below exists to serve that goal.

The technical writing process at a glance

Most guides to the stages of technical writing agree on the same core flow. Here are the seven technical writing process steps this article covers:

  1. Define your audience and their goal
  2. Choose the right type of document
  3. Plan the structure as topics
  4. Draft for scanning, not reading
  5. Review with experts and real users
  6. Publish where readers will look
  7. Maintain and improve it over time

The process is not strictly linear. Reviews often send you back to the draft, and real-world feedback often changes the structure. That is normal.

Step 1: Define your audience and their goal

Start with the reader, not the product. Google's technical writing course puts it simply: make sure your document provides the information your audience needs but doesn't already have.

To do that, answer three questions before you write a word:

  • Who is reading? A new customer, an experienced admin, a support agent, a new hire?
  • What do they already know? Familiarity with the product matters as much as job title.
  • What do they need to do after reading? Finish a setup, solve a problem, make a decision?

Watch out for the curse of knowledge. When you know a subject well, it is easy to forget what a beginner doesn't know. Steps get skipped. Jargon goes unexplained. As a rule, the wider your audience, the more you need to explain.

Step 2: Choose the right type of document

Not every question needs the same kind of page. A widely used documentation framework, Diátaxis, groups technical content into four types, each matching a different reader need:

TypeThe reader wants to…Example
Tutoriallearn by doing"Create your first project"
How-to guidereach a specific result"Export a report as PDF"
Referencelook up a fact"Account settings explained"
Explanationunderstand why"How permissions work"

A common mistake is mixing them, such as a how-to guide that wanders into long background explanation. Keep each page to one job and link to the others.

Step 3: Plan the structure as topics

Before you draft, outline. A strong approach here is topic based writing: break the documentation into small, self-contained topics, each with a clear title. The DITA standard, long used in technical communication, describes a topic as a unit of information short enough to be specific to a single subject or answer a single question, but long enough to make sense on its own.

Topics make documentation easier to:

  • Navigate, because each topic answers one question
  • Update, because a product change touches one topic, not a 40-page document
  • Reuse, because the same topic can appear in several manuals or menus

A practical way to start: list the questions your readers ask most, turn each into a topic title, then group the titles into sections. That list becomes your menu.

Step 4: Draft for scanning, not reading

Most people don't read documentation word by word. They scan. In an eyetracking study of 232 users, Nielsen Norman Group found that people often scan web pages in a rough F-shaped pattern: across the top, across again a little lower, then down the left side.

That has direct consequences for how you write:

  • Put the most important information first. State the outcome or answer in the first lines of each topic.
  • Start headings, paragraphs, and list items with words that carry meaning. Readers notice the first words of a line far more than the rest.
  • Write one idea per sentence. Short sentences are easier to read, translate, and maintain.
  • Use numbered lists for steps and bulleted lists for options. If a long sentence keeps saying "or", it probably wants to be a list.
  • Write in plain language. Choose simple words and avoid idioms and slang, which confuse international readers and translation tools. In the US, the Plain Writing Act of 2010 made writing for a specific audience a requirement for government content. It is a good standard for everyone else too.

For procedures, use a consistent pattern: one action per step, the action first, then the result the reader should see. For more on this, see our technical writing examples with before-and-after rewrites.

A note on AI. AI tools are now a normal part of many documentation workflows. In a 2026 survey of around 400 documentation professionals, a large majority reported using AI regularly or occasionally. AI can speed up a first draft or a rewrite, but it doesn't know your users or your product's edge cases. Treat AI output as a draft that still needs a subject expert and an editor.

Step 5: Review with experts and real users

Every piece of technical documentation needs two kinds of review:

  1. Accuracy review. A subject matter expert checks that every step, setting, and screenshot is correct for the current version.
  2. Clarity review. Someone close to the real audience follows the instructions exactly as written. If they get stuck, the doc needs work, not the reader.

Then edit for consistency in terminology, capitalization, and tone. A shared documentation style guide settles most of these questions once instead of in every review, and a simple technical editing checklist keeps reviews fast and repeatable.

Step 6: Publish where readers will look

Documentation only works if people can find it. That means publishing it where your readers already are, and making it easy to search:

  • Make it searchable. Clear topic titles and good headings help both your site search and search engines.
  • Make it readable on any device. Some readers will open your docs on a phone, right next to the product.
  • Use your own domain. A help center at docs.yourcompany.com builds trust and keeps readers in your brand.
  • Check readability before publishing. A readability score is a quick signal that a topic may be too dense for its audience.

Step 7: Maintain and improve it over time

Technical documentation is never finished. Every product release can make a page wrong, and outdated docs mislead the readers who trust them.

Build maintenance into the process:

  • Update docs as part of every release, not after it. Add "update the docs" to the release checklist.
  • Assign an owner to each section so someone is responsible for keeping it current.
  • Keep a version history so you can see what changed and restore earlier versions.
  • Listen to readers. Feedback on each topic shows you exactly which pages confuse people.
  • Review regularly. Set a schedule to check older topics, especially those tied to fast-changing features.

This is where a single source of truth pays off: with every topic in one version-tracked place, keeping docs current becomes routine instead of a cleanup project.

Technical documentation best practices: a quick checklist

Before you hit publish, check each topic against this list:

  • It is written for one clearly defined reader
  • It does one job: teach, guide, explain, or serve as reference
  • The title says exactly what the topic covers
  • The answer or outcome comes first
  • Steps are numbered, with one action per step
  • Sentences are short and jargon is explained
  • An expert has checked it for accuracy
  • A real user has followed it without help
  • It has an owner and a review date

Conclusion

Learning how to write technical documentation is less about talent and more about process. Know your reader, pick the right format, write focused topics for people who scan, review with experts and users, and keep everything current.
