Technical Writing

How to Build a Technical Writing Style Guide Your Team Will Actually Use

Turn scattered good instincts into consistent, scannable documentation—by writing the rules down once.

Open five pages of your documentation and you can usually tell how many people wrote them. One says “sign in,” the next says “log in,” a third says “log-in.” One page shouts in Title Case headings; another whispers in lowercase. None of it is wrong, exactly. It just doesn’t feel like one product talking.

A technical writing style guide fixes that. It’s the shared rulebook that lets a team of writers sound like a single, careful author—so readers spend their attention on your product instead of on your inconsistencies. This guide walks through what to put in one, how to build it in an afternoon, and how to keep it from going stale.

What is a technical writing style guide?

A technical writing style guide is a documented set of rules for how your team writes and structures its documentation: the voice you use, the words you prefer, how you format headings and lists, and how you organize a page. It turns hundreds of small judgment calls into decisions you make once and reuse everywhere.

Think of it as the difference between “everyone writes carefully” and “everyone writes the same careful way.” The first depends on individual talent. The second is repeatable, and it survives new hires, handoffs, and the fifth reviewer at 5 p.m.

Why a style guide is worth the effort

The case for a style guide isn’t about tidiness. It’s about how people actually read.

Readers don’t read documentation the way they read a novel. Usability research has found that most users scan a page rather than read it word by word—they hunt for the one answer they came for. The same research measured what happens when you write for that behavior: usability rose sharply when text was made concise, scannable, and plainly objective instead of promotional. Together, those changes more than doubled measured usability.

A style guide is how you make those gains the default instead of the exception. When “keep sentences short,” “front-load the key point,” and “use sentence-case headings” are written down, every author applies them without having to rediscover them. Consistency also compounds: once readers learn that your “prerequisites” always sit at the top and your steps are always numbered, they navigate faster on every page after the first.

What to put in your style guide

A useful style guide is specific enough to settle real arguments and short enough that people actually read it. Cover these sections.

Voice and tone

Describe how your documentation should sound in a few concrete rules, not adjectives. “Friendly” means nothing on its own; “write like you speak, read it aloud, and cut the jargon” gives a writer something to do. Most technical documentation lands well when it’s plain, direct, and helpful—talking to the reader, never down to them.

Pin down the small choices that come up constantly: use second person (“you”), prefer contractions (“you’re,” “it’s”), and get to the point in the first line instead of warming up to it.

Word list and terminology

The fastest consistency win is a short word list—the terms your product and industry use, spelled and capitalized one way. Decide “sign in” vs. “log in” once. Note that your product’s feature names are capitalized a specific way. List the words you ban because they’re vague or overpromise.

A living word list is also where you record the calls that used to spark debate, so they stay settled. When the same correction shows up three times in review, it belongs in the list.

Structure and topic patterns

Readability isn’t only about sentences; it’s about how a page is built. Standardize the shape of your common page types so readers learn the pattern once. A how-to might always run: goal, prerequisites, numbered steps, result. A reference page might always lead with a one-line definition.

This is where topic-based authoring helps: write documentation as modular, self-contained topics that each answer one question, rather than long chapters that bury three answers in one scroll. Self-contained topics are easier to scan, easier to reuse across manuals, and far easier to keep current—you update one topic instead of hunting for every place a fact appears. Spelling out this documentation structure in the guide keeps every author building pages the same way.

Formatting conventions

Formatting rules feel picky until you see the same page formatted three ways. Settle the recurring ones:

  • Headings: use sentence case (“Set up your account,” not “Set Up Your Account”) and skip end punctuation.
  • Lists: numbered for sequences, bulleted for everything else, with parallel phrasing.
  • Commas: pick a rule for the serial (Oxford) comma and hold to it everywhere.
  • UI and code: decide how you mark button names, menu paths, and code so readers can tell an instruction from an example at a glance.

These aren’t matters of taste so much as matters of agreement—the value is that everyone does it the same way.

Readability rules

Write down the habits that keep prose scannable. Favor short sentences and paragraphs of two or three lines. Use active voice so it’s clear who does what. Put the condition before the instruction—“To save changes, select Publish,” not “Select Publish if you want to save changes”—so readers can stop reading the moment a condition doesn’t apply to them. Start steps with a verb.

Accessibility and translation

Good documentation reaches everyone. Require descriptive alt text on every image, and descriptive link text (“read the setup guide,” not “click here”) so the page makes sense to screen readers and to anyone scanning links out of context. If you publish in more than one language, note the terms that should never be translated—your product and feature names—so meaning survives the trip.

How to build your style guide in five steps

You don’t need a hundred-page manual. You need a first version you can ship this week.

  1. Start from what you already do. Read a handful of your best existing pages and write down the patterns worth keeping. You have more conventions than you think—they’re just unwritten.
  2. Borrow, don’t reinvent. Public style guides from major software teams have already solved the common questions. Adopt sensible defaults for grammar and formatting so you can spend your effort on what’s specific to your product.
  3. Decide the contested calls. Gather the recurring arguments—the “sign in” vs. “log in” disputes—and rule on each. This is the part only your team can do.
  4. Give every rule an example. A rule with a “write this, not that” pair is followed; a rule stated in the abstract is argued about. Show the before and after.
  5. Publish it where writers work. A guide no one can find is a guide no one uses. Put it one click from the editor, not buried in a shared drive.

How to keep it from going stale

Most style guides die the same way: they’re written once, saved somewhere, and never touched again. By month two they no longer match how the team actually writes, and people quietly stop trusting them.

Three habits keep a guide alive. First, treat it as a single source of truth—one canonical version everyone points to, not five copies drifting apart in different inboxes. Second, keep its history, so you can see when a rule changed and roll back a change that didn’t work. Third, let it grow: when a reviewer makes the same correction for the third time, that’s not nagging—that’s a missing rule asking to be written down.

The takeaway

A technical writing style guide isn’t a bureaucratic hurdle—it’s how a team turns scattered good instincts into consistent, scannable documentation that readers can actually use. Start small: capture the patterns you already follow, settle the arguments that keep coming back, give every rule an example, and put it where your writers work. Then keep it living. The payoff isn’t a prettier document. It’s readers who find their answer and get on with their day.
