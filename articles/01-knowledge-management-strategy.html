How to Build a Knowledge Management Strategy That Teams Actually Use

Most teams don't lack knowledge. They lack a plan for it. The answer to a customer's question lives in one person's head, an old chat thread, and three slightly different documents. When that person goes on leave, everyone starts guessing.

A knowledge management strategy fixes that on purpose instead of by luck. This guide walks through a simple six-step plan you can write in an afternoon, with lessons from NASA's well-documented knowledge program and a one-page example you can copy.

What is a knowledge management strategy?

A knowledge management strategy is a short plan that decides which knowledge matters most to your organization, how it gets captured and shared, who owns it, and how you'll know it's working. It links everyday documentation habits to a real business goal.

It is not a software purchase. Tools support a strategy; they don't replace one.

Why a KM strategy matters more now

For years, messy knowledge was an annoyance. People worked around it by asking a colleague. That workaround is disappearing.

AI assistants and chatbots now answer questions straight from company knowledge bases. In March 2026, CX Dive reported that many companies deploying AI are discovering their knowledge management is poor, and that outdated or inconsistent content can produce wrong answers at scale. A human agent can spot two conflicting policies and pick the right one. As the article put it, "AI cannot play that referee role."

As one customer experience leader told CX Dive: "We solved a retrieval problem. We did not solve the clean data problem." A clear KM strategy is how you solve the second problem.

Step 1: Start with a business problem, not a tool

A strategy that starts with "we need a wiki" usually ends as an empty wiki. Start instead with a problem people already feel, such as:

  • New hires take months to become productive.
  • Support answers the same questions every day.
  • A key expert is retiring and nobody knows what they know.
  • Policies exist in several versions and auditors keep finding gaps.

Pick one or two. Write each as an outcome you can observe, for example "new support agents resolve tickets without help within four weeks." That outcome becomes the test for every later decision.

Step 2: Identify your critical knowledge

You can't manage everything, so don't try. NASA's knowledge management approach is built around what it calls critical knowledge: "knowledge that is essential for the agency to carry out its mission and make well-informed decisions." NASA notes that this knowledge usually comes from experience and "isn't easily replaceable."

Your version of critical knowledge is smaller, but the question is the same. Ask each team:

  1. What do we need to know to do our core work well?
  2. Which of it lives in only one or two people's heads?
  3. What would break if that person left tomorrow?

The answers give you a short, ranked list. That list is the scope of your strategy.

Step 3: Choose your mix: documents or people

This is the most important choice in any knowledge management strategy framework, and it was framed clearly in a well-known 1999 Harvard Business Review article by Morten Hansen, Nitin Nohria, and Thomas Tierney. They described two approaches:

  • Codification: capture knowledge in documents so anyone can reuse it. The authors call this a "people-to-documents" approach.
  • Personalization: connect people to the experts who hold the knowledge. These firms "focus on dialogue between individuals, not knowledge objects in a database."

The authors found that every firm they studied used both, but the effective ones focused on one and used the other "in a supporting role." Not everyone agrees. Writing in KMWorld, Professor Michael Koenig found that more successful pharmaceutical companies kept a roughly even balance, and argued that the right mix "may lie anywhere within that 80-20 to 20-80 range," and can differ between departments.

The practical takeaway: decide deliberately, team by team.

  • Lean toward documents when the same questions repeat, answers are stable, and many people need them: product manuals, help centers, policies, and onboarding.
  • Lean toward people when problems are new each time and judgment matters: complex sales deals, incident response, or research.

Most teams that write for customers or new hires will lean toward documents, with expert directories and mentoring as backup.

Step 4: Give knowledge a home and an owner

Knowledge without an owner goes stale. Knowledge in five places goes contradictory. Your knowledge management plan should name both.

  • One home. Choose a single source of truth for documented knowledge. When the same answer lives in several places, people (and AI) can't tell which one is current.
  • One owner per area. Assign each topic or collection to a named person, not a team. The owner reviews it on a set schedule and approves changes.
  • A light review step. Decide who checks new or changed content before it's published, especially for policies and customer-facing answers.

Step 5: Make capturing knowledge part of the work

This is where most strategies fail, and NASA's history shows why. In 2002, the U.S. Government Accountability Office reviewed NASA's lessons-learned efforts. Despite an agency-wide lessons-learned system that managers were expected to use, GAO found that program and project managers did not routinely identify, collect, or share lessons.

The barriers GAO cited will sound familiar: a lack of time to capture lessons, and a perceived intolerance for mistakes. GAO also said NASA's efforts were missing things leading organizations had, including strong management commitment, a well-defined business plan, incentives for sharing, and technology that makes knowledge easy to access.

NASA responded with changes such as a knowledge management strategic plan and a dedicated lessons-learned lead. Today NASA offers a knowledge management plan template and knowledge continuity guides for supervisors, departing employees, and incoming employees.

You can apply the same lessons at any size:

  • Build capture into existing moments. Add "update the docs" to the definition of done for releases, support escalations, and project close-outs.
  • Plan for departures and arrivals. Use a short handover checklist whenever someone changes roles, so critical knowledge moves before they do.
  • Make it safe to share what went wrong. Lessons from mistakes are often the most valuable, and people only share them when it won't count against them. Our guide to knowledge sharing at work covers how to make this a habit.
  • Make it easy. Let people write in tools they already know. The fewer steps between "I know this" and "it's published," the more gets captured.

Step 6: Measure use and keep improving

GAO also recommended objective performance metrics and ongoing input from users. That's good advice for any knowledge management implementation plan. Track a few signals tied to your Step 1 goal:

  • Search success: Are people finding answers, or searching and giving up?
  • Feedback: Do readers mark articles as helpful? What do their comments say is missing?
  • Freshness: How many articles are past their review date?
  • Outcome: Is the business problem improving, such as faster onboarding or fewer repeat tickets?

Review these every quarter. Retire what nobody reads, fix what readers flag, and fill the gaps they reveal. A periodic documentation audit makes this easier.

A knowledge management strategy example (one page)

Here's what a complete plan can look like for a hypothetical 40-person software company. Yours can be just as short.

ElementExample
GoalNew support agents resolve common tickets without help within four weeks.
Critical knowledgeProduct setup, top 30 customer questions, refund and escalation policies.
MixMostly documents (help center + internal guides); expert hours twice a week for edge cases.
HomeOne documentation platform for internal and customer-facing articles.
OwnersSupport lead owns policies; product manager owns setup guides; each reviews quarterly.
Capture habitsEvery escalated ticket ends with "is there an article for this?"; handover checklist for role changes.
MetricsTime to independent ticket resolution, repeat-ticket volume, article feedback, overdue reviews.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Buying a tool first. Without a goal and owners, any tool becomes a dumping ground.
  • Trying to capture everything. Start with critical knowledge and grow from there.
  • Writing it once. Knowledge has a shelf life. Without review dates, accuracy quietly decays.
  • Ignoring the culture. If sharing takes extra time or invites blame, people won't do it, no matter what the plan says.

Conclusion

A good knowledge management strategy is short and specific. It names a business problem, the critical knowledge behind it, the right mix of documents and people, a single home with clear owners, capture habits built into daily work, and a few metrics you actually check. Start with one team and one goal, prove it works, then expand.
