Runbook vs. Playbook: What's the Difference (and How to Write Both)

It's 2 a.m. An alert fires. The engineer on call has two questions in the same breath: what is actually broken here, and what exact command do I run to fix it? Those are two different questions — and in a well-run IT team, they're answered by two different documents. One is a playbook. The other is a runbook.

People use the terms as if they mean the same thing, so the wrong doc gets written, or one bloated file tries to do both jobs and does neither well. This guide draws a clear line between a runbook and a playbook, shows when to reach for each, and walks through how to write both so the person on call at 2 a.m. can act without guessing.

Runbook vs. playbook: the short answer

A runbook is a precise, step-by-step procedure for a single known task — the exact commands to restart a service, rotate a certificate, or fail over a database. A playbook is higher-level response guidance for a situation — how to assess an alert, judge its severity and impact, decide what to do, and which runbooks to run. Put simply: a playbook helps you decide; a runbook tells you do exactly this.

Both are IT documentation. Both live or die on being accurate and easy to find. The difference is altitude.

What is a runbook?

A runbook is a repeatable recipe. It assumes the decision has already been made and answers one question: how do I carry out this specific task, correctly, every time?

A good IT runbook is:

  • Task-scoped — one procedure per runbook (restart the queue worker; expand the disk; rotate the API key).
  • Command-level — each step is an action to take, not a paragraph to interpret.
  • Deterministic — follow the steps and you get the same result whether you're the author or a new hire on their first on-call shift.
  • Verifiable — it says how to confirm the task worked and how to roll back if it didn't.

Because runbooks are so concrete, they're the parts of your IT documentation most suited to automation later. Write the manual steps first and get them right; a step you can describe unambiguously is a step you can eventually script.

A minimal IT runbook template

  • Purpose — the one task this runbook performs.
  • When to run it — the trigger or the playbook that sends you here.
  • Prerequisites — access, credentials, and tools you need before you start.
  • Steps — numbered actions, one command or click each.
  • Verification — how to confirm success.
  • Rollback — how to undo it safely if something goes wrong.
  • Owner and last-reviewed date — so readers know it's still trusted.

What is a playbook?

A playbook operates one level up. It's the response guide for a type of situation — a class of alert, an incident category, a security event. Where a runbook assumes the decision is made, a playbook helps make it.

In site reliability practice, a playbook entry is typically created for every alert. It explains the alert's severity and likely impact, offers debugging suggestions, and lists the possible actions to take — including which runbooks to run. That structure is what makes on-call sustainable: it reduces stress, shortens mean time to repair, and lowers the risk of human error, because the responder isn't inventing a plan under pressure.

A strong playbook includes:

  • The trigger — the alert or symptom that opens this playbook.
  • Severity and impact — how bad this is and who it affects, so responders can prioritize.
  • Assessment steps — how to confirm what's actually happening.
  • Decision branches — a small set of paths ("if the queue is backing up, run the worker-restart runbook; if the database is unreachable, escalate and run the failover runbook").
  • Escalation and communication — who to page and what to tell stakeholders.

Keep the branches few. If a playbook grows a dozen forks, that's a signal to split it or to push detail down into separate runbooks.

They nest. An SOP might say "all production incidents follow the incident-response playbook." That playbook, mid-incident, sends you to a specific runbook. Same family, three altitudes.

When to use each

Reach for a runbook when the task is known and repeatable and you want it done the same way every time — provisioning a new environment, rotating secrets, restarting a service, running a backup restore, onboarding a server. If you can name the outcome and the steps don't really change, it's a runbook.

Reach for a playbook when judgment is involved — an alert could mean several things, an incident could unfold several ways, and someone has to assess before acting. Incident response, security events, and "the site is slow but not down" situations are playbook territory. The playbook's job is to hand the responder a clear head and a short list of runbooks, not to bury them in every command inline.

Most mature IT teams keep both, tightly linked: playbooks route to runbooks, and runbooks point back to the playbook that owns their scenario.

How to write a runbook that people trust

  1. Start from a real task. Pick something your team actually does — ideally one you did recently, so the steps are fresh.
  2. Write each step as a single action. "Run systemctl restart worker," not "restart the relevant service." Ambiguity is where 2 a.m. mistakes come from.
  3. State prerequisites up front. Access, VPN, credentials, tools. Nothing stalls a runbook like discovering halfway through that you lack permission.
  4. Add verification and rollback. Every runbook should say how to know it worked and how to back out. This is the difference between a checklist and a safety net.
  5. Test it with someone else. Hand it to a teammate who hasn't done the task. If they can follow it without asking you a question, it's done. If they can't, you found the gap.
  6. Stamp it with an owner and a review date. A runbook nobody owns is a runbook nobody trusts.

How to write a playbook that holds up under pressure

  1. Name the scenario precisely. One playbook per class of alert or incident, not a catch-all.
  2. Lead with severity and impact. The first thing a responder needs is a sense of how bad this is and who's affected.
  3. Separate assessment from action. Tell the reader how to confirm what's happening before they change anything.
  4. Keep branches shallow. A few clear paths, each ending in a runbook to run or an escalation to make.
  5. Make escalation explicit. Who to page, when, and what to say. Under stress, "use your judgment" is not guidance.
  6. Update it during the incident. The best time to improve a playbook is the moment you find it wrong or incomplete — while you're using it.

Why keeping them current is the real work

Writing the first version is the easy part. The value is in maintenance, and this is where most IT documentation quietly rots: systems change, the docs don't, and the next responder stops trusting them.

The payoff for getting it right is larger than it looks. Research into what actually drives software delivery and operations finds that documentation quality behaves like a multiplier — teams with high-quality docs get a far bigger return from every other technical practice they invest in, and reliability work in particular compounds sharply when the underlying documentation is good. Good runbooks and playbooks don't just help during one incident; they raise the ceiling on everything else the team does.

Practical habits that keep them alive:

  • Update after every use. If you touched a runbook during an incident, fix it before you close the ticket.
  • Review on a schedule. Assign owners and a cadence so stale docs surface before an incident finds them for you.
  • Track versions. Knowing what changed, when, and by whom turns your docs into a reliable record instead of a guess.
  • Keep them findable. A perfect runbook nobody can locate at 2 a.m. may as well not exist.

The takeaway

A playbook helps you decide; a runbook tells you exactly what to do. Keep them at their own altitudes, link them so one routes to the other, and — above all — keep them current in a place your whole team can reach.
