Technical Writing Examples: 7 Before-and-After Rewrites for Everyday Docs

You can spot bad documentation in seconds. Describing what makes good documentation good is harder. Most advice stays abstract: "be clear," "be concise," "know your audience." Those are good rules, but they don't show you what to change in the paragraph in front of you.

This guide takes the opposite approach. Below are seven technical writing examples, each a real-world "before" rewritten into a clearer "after." Each one comes with the principle that made the difference. By the end, you'll have a short checklist you can use on your own user manuals, help articles, and policies.

Technical writing is writing that helps a specific reader do a task or understand a product. Good technical writing is specific, easy to scan, and tells the reader what to do next.

What makes a good technical writing sample?

Before the examples, it helps to know how people actually read documentation. Mostly, they don't. In a classic Nielsen Norman Group study, 79% of test users always scanned a new web page, and only 16% read it word by word.

The same research tested rewrites of one page. Concise text, a scannable layout, and objective (non-promotional) language each improved measured usability. Combining all three improved it by 124%.

So every "after" below follows the same three ideas:

  • Lead with what the reader needs. Put the answer or the action first.
  • Make it scannable. Use short paragraphs, numbered steps, and headings that say what's inside.
  • Stay objective. Cut the hype. Readers came to get something done, not to be sold to.

Example 1: A procedure in a user manual

Procedures are the backbone of most user manuals, and the easiest place to lose a reader.

Before

In order to export a report, the Reports page should first be accessed, and the export option can then be found in the menu, after which the desired format needs to be selected and the file will be downloaded.

After

To export a report, follow these steps:

  1. In the left menu, click Reports.
  2. Open the report you want to export.
  3. Click Export > PDF. The file downloads to your computer.

Why it works: The "after" follows widely used procedure conventions, like those in Google's developer documentation style guide. A full sentence introduces the steps. Each step holds one action and uses an imperative verb ("click," "open"). The location comes before the action ("In the left menu, click…"). The step tells the reader what happens next, so they know it worked.

Example 2: A help center article intro

The first two sentences decide whether a reader stays on your help article or opens a support ticket.

Before

Welcome to our comprehensive guide! Our powerful, intuitive platform makes managing your team easier than ever, and in this article we'll walk you through everything you need to know about user roles.

After

Roles control what each person on your team can see and change. This article shows how to assign a role and what each role allows.

Why it works: The "before" spends 32 words and tells the reader nothing. The "after" defines the topic in one sentence and states what the article covers in the next. This is the inverted pyramid: conclusion first, details after. It also removes promotional language, which the NN/g research found adds a cognitive burden. Readers have to filter out the exaggeration to find the facts.

Example 3: An error message

Error messages are technical writing too. They are often the only documentation a user reads at the exact moment they're stuck.

Before

Error 4012: Invalid file. Please try again.

After

The file is larger than 25 MB. Choose a smaller file or compress it before uploading.

Why it works: The GOV.UK Design System gives some of the clearest public guidance on this. An error message should say what happened and how to fix it, in plain English. Avoid error codes, "invalid," "please," "sorry," and jokes like "oops." "Try again" fails the reader, because trying the same thing again will fail the same way.

Example 4: A field description or tooltip

Before

Visibility: Configure the visibility settings for this item.

After

Visibility: Choose who can open this page. Private pages are visible only to the people you invite.

Why it works: The "before" just repeats the label with extra words. A good description answers the question the label raises: what happens if I change this? A useful test: if your tooltip would still make sense with the label deleted, it's doing its job.

Example 5: A release note

Before

Various improvements and bug fixes were made to enhance the user experience.

After

Fixed: Tables wider than the page are no longer cut off when you export to PDF.

New: You can now duplicate a page, including its images, from the page menu.

Why it works: Release notes are read by people checking one thing: did the problem I care about change? The "after" names each change in the reader's terms and labels its type. It also uses active voice. "Improvements were made" hides who did what. "You can now duplicate a page" tells the reader exactly what they gained.

Example 6: A policy statement (plain language example)

Policies and procedures are where passive voice and legal-sounding phrasing pile up fastest.

Before

It is required that all expense claims be submitted by employees for approval no later than 30 days following the date on which the expense was incurred.

After

Submit your expense claims within 30 days of spending the money. Your manager will approve or reject each claim.

Why it works: Plain language guidance from Digital.gov stresses that active voice makes it clear who should do what, which removes ambiguity about responsibilities. Speaking to the reader as "you" does the same. The "before" never says who approves the claim. The "after" does, in 19 words instead of 27.

Example 7: A troubleshooting entry

Before

Synchronization issues may occur as a result of a variety of factors. The performance of a review of permissions should be conducted by the administrator.

After

Problem: Changes to your source document don't appear on the published page.

Fix:

  1. Check that you saved the source document.
  2. Ask your administrator to confirm you have edit access to it.
  3. Publish the page again.

Why it works: The "before" uses hidden verbs, actions turned into nouns ("the performance of a review… should be conducted"). Digital.gov calls these out as a common source of wordiness. "Review the permissions" says the same thing in three words. The "after" also uses a Problem → Fix pattern. Readers can match the symptom they see to the fix they need without reading every entry.

Types of technical writing these examples cover

The seven examples map to the types of technical writing most teams produce. Each type serves a different reading moment:

TypeWhen people read itWhat "good" looks like
User manual proceduresWhile doing the taskOne action per step, result stated
Help center articlesWhen they have a questionAnswer in the first two sentences
Error messagesWhen they're stuckWhat happened + how to fix it
UI text and tooltipsBefore making a choiceExplains the consequence
Release notesAfter an updateSpecific changes in the reader's terms
Policies and proceduresWhen rules apply to themWho does what, by when
Troubleshooting guidesWhen something breaksSymptom first, then the fix

Technical writing tips: a checklist for your own docs

Use this list on any page before you publish it:

  1. Lead with the answer. Put the definition, result, or action in the first sentence.
  2. One action per step. Say where before what: "In Settings, click…"
  3. Name the actor. Rewrite passive sentences so it's clear who does what.
  4. Find hidden verbs. Turn "conduct an analysis of" into "analyze."
  5. Cut the hype. Delete "powerful," "seamless," and "comprehensive" unless you can prove them.
  6. Make errors fixable. Say what went wrong and what to do next, with no codes and no "invalid."
  7. Check your readability score. An automated score won't make your writing good, but it flags long sentences and dense words fast.
