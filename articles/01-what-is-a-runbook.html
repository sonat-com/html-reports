What Is a Runbook? A Practical Guide to IT Runbooks

It is 3 a.m. A service is down, and the one person who knows how to bring it back is asleep, on vacation, or no longer at the company. Everyone else is guessing. This is the exact situation a runbook exists to prevent.

Runbooks turn what lives in one expert's head into steps anyone on the team can follow. By the end of this guide you will know what a runbook is, what belongs in one, how it differs from a playbook or an SOP, and how to write one your team will actually keep using.

What is a runbook?

A runbook is a documented, step-by-step guide for carrying out a specific IT task or responding to a recurring event — restoring a service, rotating a credential, onboarding a new server, or recovering after an outage. It captures one person's know-how as clear, repeatable instructions the rest of the team can follow under pressure.

The name comes from the physical binders operators once kept beside the machines they ran. The idea has not changed: write the procedure down once, in order, so the outcome does not depend on who happens to be on call.

A good runbook is narrow and specific. "Restart the payments queue worker" is a runbook. "Everything about our payments system" is not — that is a knowledge base, made up of many runbooks and reference pages.

What goes in a runbook?

The value of a runbook is in the detail. A vague procedure is almost as risky as no procedure at all. Most effective runbooks include:

  • Purpose and scope — what this runbook does, and just as importantly, what it does not cover.
  • When to use it — the trigger. Which alert, request, or symptom means "open this document."
  • Prerequisites — the access, credentials, tools, and approvals needed before starting.
  • Owner and stakeholders — who maintains the runbook and who should be notified when it runs.
  • The steps — numbered, in order, one action per step, written so a capable colleague who is not the resident expert can follow them.
  • Verification — how to confirm the task actually worked.
  • Rollback and escalation — what to do if a step fails or the situation is worse than expected.

Write for the reader who is stressed and unfamiliar, not for yourself on a calm afternoon. Spell out commands exactly. Name the dashboard. Link to the screen. Avoid jargon and assumptions — the person following the steps may be doing this for the first time.

Runbook vs. playbook vs. SOP

These three terms are used loosely and often interchangeably, which causes real confusion. The useful distinction is one of scope.

  • A runbook is task-level and precise: the exact steps to complete one well-defined operation. It answers "how do I do this specific thing?"
  • A playbook is scenario-level and strategic: how a team responds to a broader situation, such as a major incident. It coordinates people, decisions, and communication, and it usually points to several runbooks for the hands-on steps.
  • A standard operating procedure (SOP) is the organization-wide, often compliance-driven standard for how a class of work is done. Runbooks are frequently the operational expression of an SOP.

In practice they nest. During a major incident, a playbook tells the team who takes command, who communicates, and who does the technical work — while individual runbooks tell that technical responder exactly which commands to run. Established incident-management practice from site reliability engineering makes the same split: assign clear roles and keep a single living record of the response, then lean on documented, task-level procedures for the actual mitigation.

Types of IT runbooks

Most teams end up with a small library covering a few recurring categories:

  • IT operations runbooks — routine, repeatable tasks: deployments, backups, restarts, credential rotation, scaling up before a busy period.
  • Incident and disaster recovery runbooks — how to restore a service after it fails. A common pattern is a parent recovery runbook that links to child runbooks for each critical system, with recovery steps, required credentials, and troubleshooting notes for each.
  • Onboarding runbooks — provisioning a new employee's accounts and access, or standing up a new environment, without missing a step.
  • Maintenance runbooks — patching, certificate renewals, and other scheduled work that is easy to get wrong when done infrequently.

National guidance on IT contingency planning reinforces the disaster-recovery case in particular: organizations are expected to document, and regularly exercise, the procedures needed to restore systems — with the depth of planning scaled to how critical each system is.

How to write a runbook

  1. Pick one task. Keep the scope tight enough to finish in a single document.
  2. Do the task and write down every step as you go. Real-time notes beat memory. Include the commands, the exact button names, and the values you enter.
  3. Add the context around the steps. Trigger, prerequisites, owner, verification, rollback.
  4. Test it with someone else. Hand the runbook to a colleague who has never done the task and watch them follow it. Every place they hesitate is a gap to fix.
  5. Cut the ambiguity. Replace "restart the service" with the specific command. Replace "check it's healthy" with the specific dashboard and the value that means "healthy."
  6. Give it a home and an owner. A runbook nobody maintains becomes dangerous the moment reality drifts from the page.

A simple runbook template

Copy this structure for your first few runbooks. Consistency matters more than cleverness — when every runbook looks the same, responders know where to look.

Title: <Action + system, e.g. "Restart the payments queue worker">
Owner: <name / team>
Last reviewed: <date>

Purpose: <one sentence on what this achieves>
When to use: <the trigger — alert, request, or symptom>
Do NOT use this when: <out-of-scope cases>

Prerequisites:
- Access / role needed
- Tools or dashboards
- Approvals required

Steps:
1. <exact action>
2. <exact action>
3. <exact action>

Verify success:
- <how you know it worked>

If a step fails:
- Rollback: <how to undo>
- Escalate: <who to contact, and how>

A worked example makes the template concrete. For "Rotate the API signing key," the steps might be: generate the new key in the secrets manager, deploy it to staging, confirm requests still authenticate, promote to production, revoke the old key after 24 hours, and update the key's expiry reminder. Notice that every step is a single, checkable action — that is what turns notes into a runbook.

From static documents to a single source of truth

Writing the runbook is the easy part. Keeping it trustworthy is the real work. A runbook that is out of date is worse than none, because people follow it anyway.

Two habits keep runbooks healthy. First, review after every use — the moment reality differs from the page, fix the page. Second, automate the parts that are purely mechanical. When a runbook becomes a deterministic list of the same commands every time, that is a strong candidate for a script, leaving the runbook to cover judgment, verification, and the human decisions automation should not make on its own.

This is where treating documentation as a living, shared asset pays off. When runbooks live in a searchable, version-controlled home — not scattered across chat threads, personal drives, and someone's memory — they become a genuine single source of truth. The takeaway

A runbook is not bureaucracy. It is the difference between an outage that lasts minutes and one that lasts all night, and between knowledge that walks out the door and knowledge that stays with the team. Start with one task, write it so a newcomer could follow it, test it on a real person, and give it an owner. Do that a handful of times and you have the beginnings of documentation your team will actually trust when it matters most.
