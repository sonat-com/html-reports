How to Write Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs): A Practical Guide

Every team has procedures. The question is whether they live in someone's head or on the page. When the person who "just knows how it's done" is on vacation, out sick, or gone for good, undocumented knowledge becomes a risk. Standard operating procedures turn that knowledge into something anyone can follow.

This guide walks through what a standard operating procedure is, the elements every SOP needs, and a step-by-step process for writing procedures people actually use. By the end you'll have a repeatable method for turning routine work into clear, consistent documentation.

What is a standard operating procedure?

A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a set of written, step-by-step instructions that documents how to carry out a routine or repetitive task. The goal is uniformity: anyone with the right role should be able to follow the SOP and get the same result, the same way, every time.

That consistency does real work. Well-written SOPs improve efficiency and quality of output, reduce miscommunication, and help teams meet regulatory or industry requirements. They also shorten onboarding — a new hire with a good SOP can perform a task correctly on day one instead of shadowing a colleague for weeks.

SOPs sit inside the broader world of policies and procedures. A policy states what your organization does and why (for example, "all customer refunds must be approved by a manager"). A procedure — the SOP — describes exactly how to do it, step by step. You need both, but the SOP is where knowledge becomes action.

The core elements of an SOP

Formats vary by industry, but effective SOPs share a common backbone. Whatever template you adopt, make sure each procedure covers these elements.

Purpose

State why the procedure exists in a sentence or two. What outcome does it produce, and why does it matter? A clear purpose helps readers understand the intent behind the steps, so they can make sensible decisions when reality doesn't match the script exactly.

Scope

Define what the procedure covers — and what it doesn't. Which teams, systems, or situations does it apply to? Naming the boundaries up front prevents an SOP from being stretched to cover cases it was never designed for.

Roles and responsibilities

Spell out who does what. List the roles involved and the specific actions each is accountable for. When responsibilities are explicit, nothing falls through the cracks because "someone" was supposed to handle it.

The procedure itself

This is the heart of the document: the ordered steps. Break the task into discrete actions, one step per instruction, written as clear commands ("Open the billing dashboard," not "The billing dashboard should be opened"). Add the resources, tools, or access each step requires, and note any safety or compliance checkpoints.

Together these four elements — purpose, scope, responsibilities, and procedure — form a document a stranger to the task could pick up and follow. If a reader has to ask "but who does this?" or "does this apply to me?", one of the elements is missing.

How to write a standard operating procedure, step by step

With the structure in hand, here's a practical process for writing a procedure from scratch.

1. Choose the process and define the outcome

Pick one specific, repeatable task — "process a customer refund," not "handle customer service." Write down the finished result you're aiming for. A tightly scoped SOP is far more useful than a sprawling one that tries to document an entire department.

2. Talk to the people who do the work

The most accurate SOPs are written with the people who perform the task, not for them from a distance. Interview them, or better, watch them work. You'll capture the small, undocumented moves — the workaround, the double-check, the "oh, and you have to do this first" — that make the difference between a procedure that works and one that only looks right on paper.

3. Choose a format

Match the format to the complexity of the task:

  • Simple checklist — a short list of actions for straightforward, low-risk tasks.
  • Step-by-step list — numbered instructions for linear procedures with a clear beginning and end.
  • Flowchart or decision tree — for tasks with branches, where the next step depends on a condition ("if the refund is over $500, route to a manager").

Pick the lightest format that captures the task accurately. Complexity in the document should mirror complexity in the work, not exceed it.

4. Write the steps in plain language

Draft each step as a single, concrete action in the order it happens. Use plain words and short sentences, and write for the least experienced person who will ever perform the task. Avoid jargon, or define it the first time it appears. Where a screenshot, diagram, or short clip makes a step unmistakable, add it — visuals cut ambiguity, especially for frontline staff and readers working in a second language.

5. Test the procedure with a real user

Hand the draft to someone who has never done the task and ask them to follow it exactly — no hints. Every place they hesitate, guess, or go wrong is a gap in your instructions, not a failure on their part. This single test surfaces more problems than any amount of re-reading your own draft.

6. Review, approve, and publish

Before an SOP becomes official, the right people should review and approve it — a subject-matter expert for accuracy, and an owner who is accountable for the process. A defined approval step is what separates a controlled procedure from a stray document. Once approved, publish the SOP somewhere every intended reader can find it. A procedure locked in a personal drive or buried in an email thread might as well not exist.

What separates SOPs people follow from documents they ignore

Writing the first version is the easy part. Procedures fail over time for predictable reasons — and each has a fix.

They go stale. A process changes, but the SOP doesn't, so people stop trusting it and fall back on tribal knowledge. Schedule periodic reviews and record every change through version control, so you always know what changed, when, and who approved it. Keeping an archive of past versions also means you can restore an earlier one if a change turns out to be wrong.

Nobody can find them. Procedures scattered across drives, wikis, and inboxes are effectively invisible. Keep them in a single source of truth — one searchable, centralized home — so the current version is always one search away.

There's no clear owner. Every SOP needs a named owner responsible for keeping it accurate. Without one, procedures drift until they're wrong. Approval workflows help here too: routing changes through a defined reviewer keeps quality consistent as documents evolve.

They're written for the author, not the reader. If a procedure only makes sense to the person who wrote it, it isn't finished. Readability is a feature — short sentences, one action per step, and visuals where they help.

Turn routine work into reliable procedures

Good SOPs aren't bureaucracy — they're how a team scales what it already knows how to do. Start with one important, repeatable process. Cover the four elements: purpose, scope, responsibilities, and the steps themselves. Write in plain language, test the draft with a real user, and put a review-and-approval step between "draft" and "official." Then keep it current with regular reviews and version control.

Do that consistently and your procedures stop being a chore and start being an asset: faster onboarding, fewer mistakes, and knowledge that stays with the organization instead of walking out the door. Pick one process today and write the procedure — your future self, and your next new hire, will thank you.
