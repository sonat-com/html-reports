How to Write Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) That Teams Actually Follow

You already know how the work gets done. The problem is that the knowledge lives in one person's head, gets explained differently every time, and quietly disappears the day someone leaves. A standard operating procedure fixes that — but only if it's written so people can actually follow it, not filed away and forgotten.

This guide walks through how to write standard operating procedures step by step: what an SOP is, when one is worth the effort, the sections every SOP needs, and how to review, approve, and keep it current so it stays trustworthy. By the end you'll have a repeatable process you can use for any task your team does more than once.

What is a standard operating procedure?

A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a document that spells out exactly how to carry out a routine task, step by step, so it's done the same way every time — no matter who does it. Public guidance from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency describes SOPs as documents for routine or repetitive activities that exist to keep the quality and consistency of the result reliable.

That consistency is the whole point. Well-written SOPs improve communication, shorten training time, and make the output predictable — because everyone starts from the same instructions instead of their own memory.

An SOP is different from a policy. A policy says what the rule is and why ("all refunds over $500 require manager approval"). A procedure says how to carry it out, in order. Good documentation usually needs both, and they work best when they live side by side in one place your team can find.

When should you write an SOP?

Not every task deserves one. Writing SOPs for trivial or one-off work just creates documents nobody reads. Write an SOP when a task is:

  • Repeated — done regularly by more than one person.
  • Consequential — mistakes cost money, time, safety, or compliance standing.
  • Handed off — new hires, contractors, or another shift need to pick it up.
  • Audited or regulated — you need to prove the work was done a specific way.

If a process is still changing week to week, wait until it stabilizes. Documenting a moving target wastes effort and breeds distrust when the SOP is wrong within days.

How to write a standard operating procedure, step by step

Writing an SOP is less about wording and more about capturing what really happens. Follow these six steps in order.

1. Define the purpose and scope first

Before writing a single step, answer two questions in one or two sentences each:

  • Purpose: what outcome does this procedure produce? Keep it to a sentence a reader can grasp instantly.
  • Scope: who and what does it apply to — and, where it helps, what it doesn't cover?

A clear scope is the difference between a procedure people trust and one they argue about. If your scope leaves anything open to interpretation, tighten it until everyone reading it shares the same starting point.

2. Map the process with the people who actually do it

The fastest way to write a wrong SOP is to write it from your desk. Sit with the person who performs the task and watch them do it, or walk through it together. Capture every step, decision point, and "oh, except when…" exception as it comes up.

This is also where you catch steps that only survive as habit. If the expert pauses and says "then you just know to check the second tab," that's a step — write it down.

3. Choose an SOP format that fits the task

There's no single correct SOP format; match the format to the complexity of the work. Common formats include:

  • Simple steps — a short numbered list for routine tasks with few decisions.
  • Hierarchical steps — main steps with substeps, for procedures that experienced users skim and new users read in full.
  • Graphic or visual — screenshots and diagrams for anything visual or lengthy.
  • Flowchart — for decision-heavy work where the next step depends on the last answer.

Most teams standardize on one or two of these so every SOP feels familiar. A reusable template does the same job — it fixes the structure once so authors fill in content, not formatting.

4. Draft in plain, imperative language

Write the way a good instructor talks. Start each step with an action verb and address the reader directly: "Record the weight in the log," not "The weight should be recorded." Short sentences, one action per step, and no jargon or acronyms you haven't defined.

Number the steps so people can point to "step 4" out loud. Add a screenshot or a short note wherever a step is easy to get wrong. If a step needs a warning ("do not restart the machine until the light turns green"), put it before the action, not after.

5. Review, approve, and test before rollout

A draft is a hypothesis. Before it becomes the official version, put it through a short review:

  1. Peer review — the people who do the task confirm it's accurate.
  2. Expert or owner review — a manager or subject expert checks it's complete and correct.
  3. Fresh-eyes test — hand it to someone who has never done the task and watch them follow it. Every place they hesitate is a gap to fix.

This is where a documented approval workflow earns its keep. When each reviewer signs off in order, you get a clear record of who approved what and when — exactly what an auditor asks for, and exactly what stops an untested draft from going live by accident.

6. Publish, version, and keep it current

An SOP is only trustworthy if people can find the current version and know it's current. Publish it where the work happens, not in a buried folder. Give every SOP an owner and a review date, and keep a version history so you can see what changed and roll back if a change was wrong.

Review SOPs on a regular schedule and update them whenever the process itself changes. An out-of-date SOP is worse than none — it teaches the wrong thing with the authority of an official document. A single source of truth, where each procedure has one canonical home and a tracked history, is what keeps that from happening.

What should an SOP include?

Most SOPs, regardless of format, share a common backbone. Use this as a checklist of what an SOP should include:

  • Title and unique ID — a clear, descriptive name and a reference number.
  • Purpose and scope — what it achieves and who it applies to.
  • Roles and responsibilities — who performs each part.
  • Materials, tools, or access needed — everything required before starting.
  • Safety or compliance warnings — surfaced up front, where relevant.
  • The procedure — numbered steps in the order they're performed.
  • Owner, effective date, and version — so readers know it's current and who maintains it.

Not every SOP needs every element, but starting from a consistent structure means authors spend their energy on the steps, not on reinventing the layout each time.

SOP examples and templates to start from

The quickest way to get consistent SOPs is to start from a template rather than a blank page. Look at standard operating procedures examples from adjacent tasks your team already documents, then adapt the structure to the new one. A good starting template locks in the backbone above — title, purpose, scope, steps, owner — so each new SOP inherits it automatically.

If you're documenting several related procedures (say, an entire onboarding flow), keep them in one place with shared formatting and a common menu. That turns a pile of separate files into a navigable manual people can actually use.

SOP best practices

A few habits separate SOPs that get followed from SOPs that gather dust:

  • Write for the least experienced person who'll ever use it.
  • One procedure, one document — don't bury three tasks in one file.
  • Show, don't just tell — a screenshot or diagram beats a paragraph.
  • Assign an owner so every SOP has someone accountable for keeping it accurate.
  • Translate where your team is global so nobody follows a procedure in a second language they half-understand.
  • Close the loop — give readers a way to flag a step that's wrong, and fix it fast.

Bringing it together

Writing a standard operating procedure isn't a documentation chore — it's how a team turns hard-won know-how into something repeatable, teachable, and safe to hand off. Start with a clear purpose and scope, capture the real steps from the people who do the work, write them in plain imperative language, and put the draft through review before it goes live. Then keep it current, because an SOP is only as good as the last time someone checked it.
