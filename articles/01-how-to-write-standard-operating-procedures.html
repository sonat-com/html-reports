How to Write Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs) People Actually Follow

You have a process that works. It lives in one person's head, in a half-finished doc, and in the way things have "always been done." Then that person is on leave, a new hire starts, or an auditor asks how you handle something — and the gaps show. A standard operating procedure closes those gaps. By the end of this guide you'll know what an SOP needs to contain, how to write one people follow, and how to keep it from going stale.

What is a standard operating procedure?

A standard operating procedure (SOP) is a written, step-by-step set of instructions that describes how to carry out a routine task the same way every time. Its job is consistency: anyone with the right skills should be able to read the SOP and produce the same result, whether it's onboarding a customer, closing the month, or handling a returned product.

That's what separates an SOP from a policy. A policy says what your organization does and why ("we respond to every support ticket within one business day"). A procedure says how — the exact steps, in order, that make the policy real.

Why most SOPs get ignored

Plenty of teams have SOPs. Far fewer have SOPs people actually use. The usual reasons are worth naming, because each one is avoidable:

  • They're written for filing, not doing. A dense wall of text in a shared drive is technically documentation. At the moment someone needs it, it's useless.
  • They're vague. "Verify the details before approving" leaves every reader to guess what "verify" means.
  • They're out of date. The tool changed, the step didn't, and now the SOP quietly teaches the wrong thing.
  • Nobody can find them. If a procedure isn't searchable and available at the point of work, in practice it doesn't exist.

Good SOPs are written to be followed under real conditions — quickly, on whatever device is at hand, by someone who is mid-task and not in the mood to decode jargon.

What every SOP should contain

Before you write steps, decide on a consistent structure. Environmental and quality-management guidance on preparing SOPs converges on a common backbone — this kind of header-plus-body format — and it holds up well outside the lab:

  • Title and a unique ID. A clear name and an identifier so the document can be referenced and tracked.
  • Version and status. Which revision this is, and whether it's a draft or approved. This is the anchor for everything in the next section.
  • Purpose and scope. One or two lines on what the procedure covers and, just as useful, what it does not.
  • Roles. Who performs each step and who is accountable for the outcome.
  • Prerequisites. Tools, access, materials, or safety requirements needed before starting.
  • The procedure itself. Numbered steps in the order they happen.
  • References. Related policies or procedures, kept as internal metadata.

You don't need every field for every task. A three-step reset procedure shouldn't carry the same overhead as a regulated compliance process. Match the structure to the risk.

How to write a standard operating procedure, step by step

1. Scope the procedure first

Write down where the procedure starts and where it ends before anything else. A common mistake is trying to document an entire department in one file. Instead, capture one task with a clear trigger ("a refund request is received") and a clear finish ("the customer is notified and the ledger is updated"). Tight scope is what makes an SOP followable.

2. Talk to the people who do the work

The best source for a procedure is the person who performs it every day, not the manager who thinks they know how it's done. Watch the task once, then draft from what actually happens — including the small checks and workarounds that never made it into anyone's notes. SOP preparation guidance is consistent on this point: procedures should be written by people knowledgeable in the activity, then reviewed by others who do it.

3. Write for the point of use

This is where most SOPs live or die. A few habits make the difference:

  • Use the active voice and name the actor. "The reviewer approves the invoice" beats "the invoice should be approved." The reader always knows who does what.
  • One action per step. If a step contains an "and," it's probably two steps.
  • Use concrete verbs. Measure, record, send, confirm — not "handle" or "process."
  • Add visuals where a picture is faster than a sentence. A screenshot or a simple diagram often removes an entire paragraph of explanation.

4. Choose a format that fits the task

Not every procedure is a flat list. Pick the shape that matches the work:

  • Simple steps for short, linear tasks.
  • Hierarchical steps when a step needs sub-steps or detail for newer staff.
  • A flowchart when the path branches on decisions ("if the order is over $500, route it for a second approval").

Consistency across your library matters more than picking the "perfect" format for any one document. Readers learn one shape and move faster through all of them.

Control, review, and keep SOPs current

An SOP that no one maintains becomes a liability — it teaches the old way with full confidence. This is exactly the problem that document control solves, and it's a point every serious guide to writing SOPs agrees on: approve a procedure before it's used, review and update it on a schedule, record each revision, make the current version available where the work happens, and archive obsolete versions so no one follows them by mistake.

In practice that means four habits:

  • Approve before publishing. A defined approval workflow — sequential or parallel — makes sure the right people sign off before a procedure goes live.
  • Version everything. Keep a history so you can see what changed, when, and by whom, and restore an earlier version if a change was wrong.
  • Set a review cadence. How often should standard operating procedures be reviewed? Review periodically — many teams set an annual check — and always after the underlying process, tool, or regulation changes, whichever comes first.
  • Retire the old copy. When a new version is approved, the previous one should stop circulating so no one follows outdated steps.

A simple SOP template to start from

You don't need special software to begin. Start with this skeleton and fill it in:

  1. Title / ID / Version — name it, number it, mark the revision.
  2. Purpose & scope — what this covers and where it stops.
  3. Roles — who does it, who's accountable.
  4. Prerequisites — access, tools, materials, safety.
  5. Steps — numbered, one action each, active voice.
  6. Review — owner and review date.

Draft one procedure with it this week. A single clear, followed SOP is worth more than a binder of documents nobody opens.

Common SOP mistakes to avoid

  • Documenting the ideal instead of the real. Capture how the work is actually done, then improve it — don't skip to the fantasy version.
  • Writing once and walking away. "Write it and forget it" is how SOPs drift out of date; a review date is not optional.
  • Burying it. If people can't search and open it in seconds, they'll improvise.
  • Over-documenting. Ten pages for a two-minute task guarantees no one reads it.

Start with one procedure

Great standard operating procedures aren't a writing project — they're a habit: scope one task, capture how it's really done, write it in plain steps for the person at the point of work, and put it under review so it stays true. Do that once, notice how much smoother the handoff gets, and repeat.
